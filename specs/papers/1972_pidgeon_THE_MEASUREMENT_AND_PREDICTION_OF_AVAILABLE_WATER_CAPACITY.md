# THE MEASUREMENT AND PREDICTION OF AVAILABLE WATER CAPACITY OF FERRALLITIC SOILS IN UGANDA

J. D. PIDGEON$^{1}$

*(Department of Soil Science, Makerere University, Kampala, Uganda)*

## *Summary*

Field capacity was measured directly, and soil moisture characteristics were determined on undisturbed cores, for a wide textural range of ferrallitic soils in Uganda. The initial moisture conditions of the soils were shown to affect results and thus standardized procedures were adopted for field and laboratory determinations. Laboratory estimates of field capacity for undisturbed and disturbed samples were shown to be unreliable, but a correction factor was found which improved them. There was no single moisture tension for undisturbed core samples that corresponded to field capacity.

Particle-size composition could be related to field capacity, permanent wilting-point, and available water capacity by multiple regression equations having correlation coefficients of 0.96, 0.98, and 0.88 respectively. The relationships predicting available water capacity were different from those found for soils in England, but those for field capacity and permanent wilting-point were similar. Particle-size analyses were carried out using three methods of dispersion of different efficiencies. Particle-size composition following gentle dispersion by shaking soil with distilled water was poorly correlated with moisture-holding properties. There was little advantage in using vigorous ultrasonic dispersion compared with overnight shaking with sodium hexametaphosphate.

Two soils formed on alluvial deposits with kaolinite the only clay mineral, and one soil with montmorillonite the dominant clay mineral, showed markedly different relationships between moisture-holding properties and particle-size composition.

## *Introduction*

OVER most of Uganda the lack of available water in the soil is an important limiting factor to crop growth throughout most of the year. Irrigation must play an increasingly important part in attempts to intensify and diversify agricultural production. Almost all the detailed studies in the literature on soil water availability relate to soils where 2:1 lattice clays predominate. This paper examines the validity, for ferrallitic soils, of the generally accepted methods of measuring the limits of available water, and determines the most accurate, and the most cost-effective, methods of predicting these properties.

## *Choice of soils*

Eleven sites were chosen to include a wide textural range of soils from loamy sands to clays (Table 2). Seven soils were classified as ferrallitic (Chenery, 1959) and kaolinite was the only clay mineral present. At two sites at Entebbe the soils were formed on alluvial deposits and kaolinite was the sole clay mineral. At one site (Serere 1) the soil was

ferrallitic, but the dominant clay mineral was Illite; at another (Nab-bongo) the soil was forming on relatively recent volcanic material, clay fraction mainly montmorillonite. All soils had been under cultivation for a few years, and most were in the first-year ley of a three-year arable, three-year ley rotation.

All soils were sampled at four depths of 6–9 cm, 21–4 cm, 37–40 cm, and 51–4 cm.

### *Experimental methods*

#### *The field method for field capacity*

Most workers using the field method recommend sampling 48 h after thorough wetting of the profile, though field capacity (FC) is not an equilibrium condition (Childs and Collis-George, 1948) and several studies (e.g. Van Bavel *et al.*, 1968) have shown that slow drainage may take place for several weeks. Preliminary experiments at Kabanyolo on the deep clay loam soil—thought *a priori* to be the slowest draining of the soils due to its heavy texture and relatively poor structure—confirmed that 48 h was an acceptable time to allow for drainage. Two 3 m × 3 m plots on almost level land (slope < 2°) were wetted to a depth of at least 2 m by addition of 30 cm of water without any surface run-off. Both plots were sampled in duplicate at the four depths previously mentioned at intervals of 12 h for 5 days. Loss of water was not detectable after 36 h.

Several workers have reported seasonal differences in moisture contents at FC for soils in England (e.g. Salter and Williams, 1965) and for highly weathered soils in the tropics (e.g. Kowal, 1969) which are attributed to hysteresis effects, though soil temperature changes may also have been important.

The effect of the initial conditions of the soil on moisture contents at FC was therefore studied, as seasonal contrasts in moisture regimes of soils over most of East Africa are very marked. A small plot, 3 m × 3 m, and its surrounds, was cleared at Kabanyolo in March 1971, towards the end of an exceptionally severe dry season when the top metre of the profile, at least, had been at permanent wilting-point (PWP) or drier for a minimum of 2 months. The plot was wetted to a depth of at least 2 m and FC determined by direct sampling at 6–9 cm and 51–4 cm after 48 h, the plot being covered by a sheet of polythene to prevent evaporation. FC was then redetermined after 12, 30, and 90 days on the same plot at the same depths, the plot being lightly watered daily except for the 2 days prior to each determination.

Longer periods of wetting than 90 days were not investigated because the climate of most of Uganda ensures that such conditions do not occur in the field. The results are shown in Table 1, each moisture content being the mean of six determinations.

The results are non-significant for the A horizon, partly due to high intra-horizon variability, but for the B horizon differences are large enough to have a considerable effect on AWC values. This confirms, for ferrallitic soils, the point that if values of FC, or more importantly

After thorough wetting to at least 2 m, all plots were watered lightly once a day for 10 days and allowed to drain for 2 days, before FC was determined. This was, however, a time- and labour-consuming process.

### *Moisture characteristics*

Moisture release characteristics were determined on ‘undisturbed’ 3 cm thick × 4·7 cm diam core samples in the 0-1 bar range using the pressure plate apparatus of Soil Moisture Equipment Co. of California. Three core samples were taken at each depth from each site. All cores were wetted under vacuum in order to minimize alterations in soil structure, during wetting, caused by entrapped air (Smith and Browning, 1948). Preliminary experiments showed, as expected in the light of work outlined above, that time of wetting had a marked effect on moisture characteristics. The same time of sampling, and a similar wetting procedure, to that described for the direct method for FC were adopted, in order to make comparisons between laboratory estimates and field values of FC meaningful. Core samples were taken at the end of the rainy season, stored in a moist atmosphere for the shortest possible time (0-2 weeks) and soaked for 10 days before moisture characteristics were determined. An additional advantage of sampling soon after the end of the wet season was that the soils were not hard and hence a minimum of force was required in taking samples.

The Soil Moisture Equipment Co. catalogue no. 1500 pressure plate was used for measurements on the same core samples in the 1-15 bars range. However, no satisfactory method could be found for equilibrating cores from the heavier soils at a tension of 15 bars. Constant weight was not attained after 18 days, and covering the porous plate with a thin layer of finely ground loam soil and a piece of muslin to improve capillary contact with the core samples (Richards, 1954) appeared to have no effect. Longer equilibration times were considered prohibitive for routine measurements, and this method for determining PWP was therefore rejected in this case in view of the impossibility of making a rational choice of equilibrium time.

### Disturbed sample estimates of FC and PWP

The moisture contents of samples equilibrated at 1/3 bar were determined by the method described in the USDA Handbook No. 60 (1954), except that samples were not ground and sieved. As three replicates were taken at each depth there seemed no point in alteration of soil structure to achieve uniformity. 25 g samples (approximately 5 mm thick) were soaked overnight on the plates, and a pressure of 1/3 bar then applied for 48 h. Outflow of water invariably ceased within this time. The samples were then rapidly transferred to moisture tins and their moisture contents determined gravimetrically.

Disturbed samples used for estimating PWP from moisture contents at 15 bars tension were soaked for 10 days, since they were to be compared with FC values from the direct method, described previously, to obtain values for AWC. There was some field evidence to justify this procedure, which is discussed later. Subsequent operations followed the method outlined in the previous paragraph, but using a pressure of 15 bars and the appropriate pressure plate.

### Particle-size analysis

Particle-size analysis usually involves arbitrarily chosen dispersion procedures. In this study separate particle-size analyses were carried out following three methods of dispersion, to determine which method of dispersion yielded results best correlated with soil moisture properties of ferrallitic soils.

The first, most efficient, method involved destruction of organic matter by hydrogen peroxide, followed by ultrasonic dispersion for 15 min using a 20 cm × 10 cm tank, giving 300 W at 25 kHz. Ten per cent sodium hexametaphosphate solution was added immediately after dispersion to stabilize the suspensions (some samples from B horizons otherwise rapidly reflocculated). Silt and clay were determined by the pipette method and the sand was fractionated into three size ranges—coarse sand 2000–200 μm, fine sand 200–50 μm, and very fine sand 50–20 μm.

The second method involved shaking the soil with water overnight on a reciprocating shaker, and using sodium hexametaphosphate as dispersing agent. Organic matter was not destroyed prior to shaking. Silt and clay were estimated by the hydrometer method and the percentage of sand obtained by subtraction.

The third method employed less violent dispersion techniques in an attempt to gain some insight into the sorts of particle-size distributions that might exist in these soils under conditions closer to those in the field. A similar technique has been used by Ahn (1968) on similar soils in West Africa where micro-aggregation is also important. Samples were shaken in water for half an hour on the reciprocating shaker, with a soil:water ratio of 1:5. No dispersing agent was used. The samples were shaken in the measuring cylinders in which the suspensions were to be left to stand, so that washing of soil from one container to another, with attendant possibilities of additional non-reproducible dispersion,

was eliminated. Immediately after shaking, suspensions were made up to 990 ml and 10 ml of 5 per cent sodium hexametaphosphate solution added to prevent reflocculation. The suspensions were not stirred in the presence of Calgon as this visibly induced further dispersion. Results were found to be reproducible to $\pm 2$ per cent, and variability of this degree may well be due to natural variation in soils. Samples were analysed in duplicate and prior to this were stored moist as early experiments showed that even air-drying had some effect on the percentages of clay and silt found.

Organic matter determinations were carried out by the Walkley-Black method, assuming 77 per cent oxidation of organic carbon and using a factor of 2 to convert organic carbon to organic matter. Total nitrogen was determined by the Kjeldahl digestion method. Bulk density measurements were made by the core method, as all the soils in this study were stone free. Values for the lower three depths are means of three measurements, while values for the uppermost, and more variable, horizon are means of six determinations.

### *Results and discussion*

#### *Validity of laboratory methods of measuring FC and PWP*

Table 2 shows the values of FC measured in the field, compared with values obtained from three commonly used laboratory estimates, namely the moisture contents at $\frac{1}{10}$ bar and $\frac{1}{3}$ bar for 'undisturbed' cores and at $\frac{1}{3}$ bar for disturbed samples. None of these three laboratory estimates is satisfactory, as was found by Salter and Williams (1965). Each has a correlation coefficient with FC by the direct method of approximately 0.8 and the percentage variation unexplained is too great to allow useful estimates of FC to be made. Also, for 'undisturbed' core samples from these soils, there is no one tension that will give moisture contents comparable to those at FC. In fact, moisture tensions equivalent to FC range from below $\frac{1}{10}$ bar to approximately $\frac{1}{3}$ bar, with no clear pattern of variation with clay content. Good agreement has been found between FC and $\frac{1}{3}$-bar moisture contents of undisturbed cores from the B horizon of the Kikuyu loam soil at the East African Agriculture and Forestry Research Organization, Muguga (P. H. Hosegood—personal communication). In this case large (10 cm diam) cores were used. This may be important in obtaining a sample the structure of which is representative of that of the soil in the field. Table 2 shows that good agreement was occasionally found in the present study, but that this must be regarded as the exception rather than the rule.

An attempt was made to find an adequate equation predicting FC from laboratory measurements. Greatly improved correlations were obtained when a constant was introduced into the regression equations giving:

The above equations may be widely applicable to the improvement of laboratory estimates of FC. As the correlation coefficients quoted above are not significantly different, and the disturbed-sample method is considerably simpler than the use of undisturbed cores, clearly when a laboratory method has to be used to estimate FC, the disturbed sample method, with values corrected by Equation 3 above, is the most cost-effective method.

Several workers (e.g. Salter and Williams, 1965; Wilcox, 1960) have shown that the 15-bar per cent moisture content of undisturbed samples

is a reasonable approximation to PWP. For reasons outlined earlier, it proved impossible to measure this satisfactorily, and disturbed samples that had been air-dried and were then soaked for 10 days were used instead. At three sites (Kabanyolo, Serere 1 and 2) 15-bar per cent moisture contents were measured on disturbed samples that had been oven-dried and soaked only overnight, following the procedure of Richards (1954). At these same three sites the actual soil moisture contents at the appropriate depths were measured by the gravimetric method in March 1971, towards the end of an exceptionally severe dry season when the vegetation had been wilted for several weeks. These moisture contents at PWP, determined under field conditions, agreed within $\pm 0.5$ per cent w/w of water with laboratory determinations on air-dried samples that had been soaked for 10 days. They were appreciably higher (approximately $+3$ per cent w/w of water) than laboratory results for oven-dried soils soaked overnight. This result lends support to the modified soaking procedures that were adopted. It also emphasizes the importance of avoiding, or compensating for, laboratory procedures such as drying which alter the moisture conditions of soils prior to wilting-point determinations.

Regression equations were first calculated using data from the 27 horizons (6 sites $\times$ 4 depths each + 3 depths at Kabanyolo 2) in which the clay fraction was almost entirely kaolinite. Data from the soil at Serere 1, where the clay fraction was predominantly illite, fitted these regression equations without any significant changes in constants or correlation coefficients. However, inclusion of data from the soils at Entebbe (clay fractions kaolinitic but parent materials alluvial) caused large, highly significant falls in the values of all correlation coefficients, as did inclusion of data from the soil at Nabbongo (volcanic parent material and clay fraction predominantly montmorillonite). These two groups of soils were therefore excluded as having quite different relationships between particle-size composition and moisture-holding properties. Regression equations were therefore recalculated for 31 horizons (the original 27 + 4 at Serere 1) and apply only to non-alluvial soils in Uganda whose clay fractions are kaolinitic or possibly illitic.

Each of the four dependent variables was calculated on a weight and a volume basis. Correlation coefficients with particle-size composition data were consistently and significantly higher when FC and PWP were expressed on a weight basis, and hence bulk density must be measured separately. For AWC and EAWC, correlation coefficients were not significantly different when these properties were expressed on a volume or a weight basis, and hence a volume basis was preferred.

Inclusion of organic matter percentage (OM) as an independent variable in the regression equations significantly improved the fit in most cases (Table 3). Inclusion of total nitrogen percentage instead had an almost identical effect despite small differences in C/N ratios between soils, and between topsoils and subsoils. In view of this, use of OM (= 2 $\times$ organic carbon) was preferred.

Subdivision of the sand fraction into three size fractions: coarse sand (2000–200 $\mu$m), fine sand (200–50 $\mu$m), and very fine sand (50–20 $\mu$m) in particle-size analysis by method 1 (ultrasonic dispersion) did not result in improved regression equations. When the three sand fractions were included as independent variables, invariably at least one regression coefficient had a non-significant t-value. Equations involving two of the sand fractions were not improved by introduction of the independent variable OM, and fitted less well than equations involving total sand, or clay, and OM. This finding is in contrast with the results of Salter *et al.* (1966) for soils in England, as also is the result that coarse sand, as well as fine sand, is positively correlated with AWC.

Significantly lower (although frequently still highly significant—Table 3) correlation coefficients were found when particle-size data obtained by method 3 were correlated with moisture-holding properties. Clearly for investigations of this nature, vigorous methods of dispersion must be used. In most cases there were no significant differences between correlation coefficients obtained using data from particle-size analysis methods 1 and 2, although in general, method 1 using ultrasonic dispersion tended to give clay percentages between 5 and 10 per cent

higher. Usually sand or clay percentages by method 1 were slightly better correlated with moisture-holding properties than values from method 2. However, when organic matter percentage was introduced into the regressions it improved the fit of method 2 data more than that of data from method 1 and equivalent 2-factor regressions were usually not significantly different. An important exception is in prediction of AWC where a significantly better fit is obtained using clay percentage from method 1 than method 2—see later.

For FC and PWP, correlation coefficients of regression equations using silt and clay were slightly superior to those of equations using sand percentage. However, differences in the correlation coefficients never approached significance at the 5 per cent level. For AWC and EAWC the best regression equations involving sand percentage were not improved by introduction of organic matter values, and were significantly inferior to 3-factor regressions involving silt, clay, and organic matter.

They are all slightly better fitting (accounting for an extra 9 per cent of the variation on average) than the equivalent equations of Salter and Williams (1969). This may be because in this study the ferrallitic soils tend to be relatively uniformly weakly structured. Hence variations in structure (which are not taken into account directly) may contribute somewhat less to the total variation in moisture-holding properties than for soils in England. The equations predicting FC and PWP are fairly similar to those of Salter and Williams, though FC values tend over all to be somewhat lower. A possible explanation for this difference is that the higher soil temperatures prevalent in Uganda reduce the viscosity, and, to a lesser extent, the surface tension, of water. This would tend to reduce moisture contents at FC, particularly if FC is not an equilibrium condition but depends on rates of drainage, and hence on viscosity.

In three cases out of four the best possible equations were those involving method 2. As this method requires no expensive or sophisticated apparatus it is clearly considerably more cost-effective. The exception was prediction of AWC where the best equation involved clay

In many cases this will provide a satisfactory estimate and the purchase of ultrasonic dispersion apparatus may not be justifiable. There is only a small loss in accuracy if all four soil moisture properties are predicted from equations involving only sand (2) percentage and organic matter (Table 3). As sand can be determined by a single hydrometer reading or pipette sampling after approximately 4 min sedimentation time, this procedure is extremely rapid.

### Conclusions

A study of ferrallitic soils in Uganda showed that:

1. Moisture contents at FC depend on previous moisture conditions.
2. Laboratory determinations of FC are unreliable. Correction factors are given which may improve laboratory estimates.
3. Laboratory procedures for measuring PWP should avoid, or compensate for, prior air drying of samples.
4. FC, PWP, AWC, and EAWC may be predicted directly from particle-size analysis data and organic matter contents.
5. There is no advantage gained in prediction of soil-moisture properties by subdivision of the sand fraction in particle-size analysis.
