# CHAPTER I

## INTRODUCTION

Irrigation and drainage engineers are faced with the problem of designing irrigation and drainage systems such that there is proper maintenance of moisture, air and salt balance for favorable plant growth. A major consideration in the design is getting water into or out of the soil. In either case, the flow phenomenon involved is flow through partially saturated porous media. When water enters a soil, air must be replaced; and when water is removed, air must enter. The flow, therefore, involves two immiscible fluid phases, air and water. Frequently, these problems are divided into two regimes of flow, (1) above the water table and (2) below the water table. Water table is defined as the locus of points where the pressure of the water is zero (gage) and is therefore a convenient reference datum. Two distinguishing characteristics of the two regimes of flow are: (1) Above the water table there exist functional relationships among saturation, pressure difference between air and water, and the permeabilities to air and water; and (2) below the water table saturation and permeability are constants.

Under static conditions or conditions of no flow, one may bore a hole into the soil to determine the approximate location of the water table. The depth at which water is found in the hole is a point on the water table. In the process of boring, one notices that water does not flow into the hole until the water table is reached. This observation has led many to believe a sharp discontinuity exists in the moisture content of soils at the water table. Thus, many solutions

to irrigation and drainage problems have been proposed ignoring flow above water tables. For example, many investigators, including this author, Brooks (4), have formulated and solved differential equations dealing with the flow of water through soils to drain tile and they have (with rare exceptions) completely ignored the flow above the water table in order to simplify the problem. Such assumptions in most cases are not realistic. In real cases, there is no sharp discontinuity in the soil moisture content at the water table.

If engineers are to solve problems of flow in porous media with some degree of accuracy, the entire flow system must be considered whether it includes the flow above or below the water table or both. This realistic approach requires at least a knowledge of the functional relationship among the variables in the region above the water table since it is the most difficult to visualize in formulating problems and presents formidable barriers in solving these flow problems analytically.

The main objective of this thesis is to develop some general theoretical relations that describe how the variables are interrelated and to determine from them, if possible, certain intrinsic properties of porous media that affect them. Some new methods for measuring the pertinent properties are also described. The information presented is concerned only with substantial flow of fluids in bulk at relatively high liquid saturations. The slow migration of liquid at saturations less than field capacity is not described by the theory or data presented. An attempt is made to present the information in a manner which will be useful to engineers.

Prediction equations for any phenomenon involve the use of certain parameters that are characteristic of the phenomena in question.

Regardless of how well the equations describe the physical situation or how accurate the methods of solution are, the prediction of the phenomena can be no more realistic than the measurement and description of these parameters. Flow of fluids in partially saturated porous media is no exception to this fact. Very often, however, boundary conditions are so complex that it is impossible to predict the behavior of the system even when the intrinsic characteristic constants along with the differential equation describing the system are known. One may logically resort to a physical model of the system to determine its complicated behavior, provided similitude requirements can be satisfied.

If the information in this thesis cannot presently be included in design equations, at least it will help guide the construction of valid models of systems involving flow in partially saturated media.

## CHAPTER II

### BACKGROUND

Much of the literature describing flow of two immiscible fluids in porous solids is in the fields of petroleum technology and soil physics. Because the language in some of the literature is unfamiliar and because much of it is qualitative in nature, drainage and irrigation engineers have not made use of the available information concerning two-phase flow.

The purpose of this chapter is to bring together well established theories and facts developed in these fields by engineers and scientists that will contribute to an understanding of the theory presented in Chapter III. The theoretical development in Chapter III is an extension of the present theoretical status of two-phase flow in porous media. Most of the background for this theory is derived from the field of petroleum technology.

The author has taken the liberty to convert all terms and symbols from the various papers cited into standard nomenclature consistent with the theoretical development in Chapter III.

#### Basic Definitions

In the two-fluid system discussed in this thesis and by the other investigators cited, the fluids are assumed to be immiscible. They are referred to as nonwetting and wetting phases. Two-phase experiments have been conducted with a variety of liquids, e. g. air-oil, air-water, mercury-air, etc. In each instance one fluid phase wets the solid surface and the other does not.

A convenient way to determine which phase is wetting or nonwetting is to visualize the two fluids in a capillary tube. Since

they are immiscible, they will be separated by an interface. At the interface, the curvature is always concave toward the wetting fluid.

Darcy's equation (14) is assumed to apply to the flow of both wetting and nonwetting phases occupying a porous medium. For this assumption to be valid, both phases must form continuous networks within the medium and any isolated bubbles of air (in the case of an air-water system) must be regarded as part of the porous matrix. Darcy's equation is written as:

$$q_{x} = \frac{-K_{ex}}{\mu} \left[ \frac{\Delta P}{L_{x}} + \frac{\gamma \Delta h}{L_{x}} \right]$$

in which $q_{x}$ is the component of volume flux in the x-direction, $\gamma$ is the specific weight of the fluid, $\mu$ is the viscosity, $\Delta h$ and $\Delta P$ are the difference in elevation and pressure respectively over the distance $L_{x}$ in the x-direction, and $K_{ex}$ is the effective permeability in the x-direction. If the medium contains only one fluid, $K_{ex}$ is a constant independent of the pressure and it is given the symbol $K_{x}$. However if the medium contains two immiscible fluids, the effective permeability is not independent of pressure but rather it is a function of the pressure difference between the two phases.

Darcy's equation has been written in the most general form and no restriction is made concerning isotropy of the medium or whether the pores of the medium are only partially filled with the wetting phase. The permeability, K, in equation 1 has been called by Richards (25) "intrinsic permeability" since for stable media

it is an intrinsic property of a medium and is independent of the fluid properties used to make the measurement. It has dimensions of length squared.

The fraction of the total pore space occupied by the wetting phase is defined as saturation, $S$. It is assumed to be a quantity which can vary from point to point within the medium. It is expressed as:

$$S = \frac{\Delta v_t}{\Delta v_p} \quad 2$$

where $\Delta v_t$ is an increment of fluid volume occupying a portion of an element of pore volume, $\Delta v_p$, of a small element, $\Delta v$, of the bulk medium.

When a wetting and nonwetting phase coexist in a porous medium, a pressure difference exists across the interfaces separating the two phases. This pressure difference is called capillary pressure, $P_c$. In symbols,

$$P_c = P_{nw} - P_w \quad 3$$

where the subscripts $nw$ and $w$ denote nonwetting and wetting phases respectively. An expression showing how capillary pressure is related to the radii of curvatures of the curved interfaces and to the interfacial tension, $\sigma$, is

$$P_c = \sigma \left[ \frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2} \right] \quad 4$$

where $r_1$ and $r_2$ are major and minor radii of curvatures at a point on the interface. A derivation of equation 4 has been presented by Adams (1).

## Basic Assumptions

For purposes of discussion in the remainder of this chapter and throughout the thesis, it will be assumed that the nonwetting phase is a gas and the wetting phase is a liquid and that the wetting phase will be at a pressure less than the pressure of the gas. The assumption is also made that curved interfaces exist over a wide range of saturations and that the definitions given by equations 3 and 4 are valid at least from complete saturation to field capacity. Field capacity has been defined by Israelsen (18) as the saturation value that a deep soil profile reaches when allowed to drain for 2 - 5 days after a heavy irrigation. Obviously, an equivalent value of saturation cannot be determined in the laboratory, however, it is a moisture condition in the soil that engineers and agronomists frequently refer to.

This chapter and succeeding chapters will be primarily concerned with desorption or drainage of liquid from media. When a gas displaces a liquid from a porous medium (by gradually increasing $P_c$), the first liquid displaced is that occupying the largest pores in contact with the gas. As $P_c$ is further increased, the interfaces retreat into successively smaller spaces. The saturation, S, and the effective permeability, $K_e$, are both, therefore, functions of $P_c$.

## Flow in Straight Tubes of Irregular Cross Section

Flow in thin films and very small tubes has much in common with flow in porous solids. The solutions of the Navier-Stokes equation for laminar flow through small diameter tubes and film flow

over an infinite flat plate have been used to obtain an equation analogous to Darcy's Law for saturated flow through porous media.

The equation of Poiseuille (22) for mean steady velocity, $\overline{u}$, through a small tube of diameter, $r_t$, is

$$\overline{u} = - \frac{r_t^2}{8\mu} \nabla P^* \tag{5}$$

where $\mu$ is the viscosity, and $\nabla P^*$ is the gradient of $P + \gamma h$. Similarly, the equation for flow over an infinite flat plate is

$$\overline{u} = - \frac{d^2}{3\mu} \nabla P^* \tag{6}$$

where d is the thickness of the film.

One notes the similarity in form of equations 5 and 6. The mean velocity is proportional to $\nabla P^*$ and to the square of a length dimension characterizing the area of flow. It is also inversely proportional to viscosity and a numerical constant.

By induction, an analogous equation applying to straight tubes of uniform cross section other than circular is

$$\overline{u} = - \frac{R^2}{k\mu} \nabla P^* \tag{7}$$

where R is a length characterizing the size of the tube and k is a numerical constant depending on the shape of the cross section and the dimension chosen to characterize the size. Carman (9) called the quantity R the mean hydraulic radius. Equation 7 is Carman's general law of streamline flow through straight irregular channels.

### The Concept of Hydraulic Radius

Hydraulic radius is defined as the cross-sectional area normal to the flow divided by the wetted perimeter. When equations

5 and 6 are rewritten in terms of hydraulic radius, Poiseuille's equation becomes

$$\overline{u} = - \frac{R^2}{2\mu} \nabla P^*$$ 8

and equation 6 becomes

$$\overline{u} = - \frac{R^2}{3\mu} \nabla P^*$$ 9

The variation in the numerical constant is evidently greatly reduced when equation 7 is written in terms of hydraulic radius.

It is reasonable to suppose that (from a hydrodynamic viewpoint) flow in a thin film is about as different from flow in a circular tube as is possible to obtain. One might conclude, therefore, that the numerical constant, k, in equation 7 for irregular cross sections of flow should be somewhere between 2 and 3. This constant, k, called shape factor, has been studied by many investigators (among the earliest were Fair and Hatch (16); and it has been found experimentally to have values ranging from 1.8 - 2.5, depending upon the shape of the cross sections (Carman (9).

This conclusion would not be valid, however, if the cross section has an extreme "eccentricity". Consider, for example, a cross section as shown in Figure 1. Note the perimeter of the long extension is great but the area is small and the contribution to flow of this part of the area is, relatively, even smaller.

The difficulty is that the distribution of velocity in one part of the cross section is like that in a relatively large tube, whereas in the extension, it is like that between closely spaced plates. The large part of the eccentric cross section, therefore,

would act as a tube with k ≈ 2 whereas the extension would act as a tube with k ≈ 3. The larger part, however, has a much larger hydraulic radius than the extension. Note that in equation 7, R is squared and k is to the first power. One cannot, therefore, use R for the entire cross section and an average of k to obtain a valid estimate of ū.

It is much more reasonable to regard the eccentric cross section shown in Figure 1 as two separate cross sections. The error resulting from applying an average k of about 2.5 for each cross section will not be great, but it is necessary to use separate values of R. An alternative method (which will give exactly the same result) is to consider the entire cross section as a whole, using an average k and a mean value of R², designated as R̄².

The tubes considered in developing equation 7 were straight. There is no difficulty in extending the analysis to other than straight tubes provided that the actual length of the tubes can be measured and the direction that any fluid element is moving at any particular location is known. There is an error in such an analysis if the normal acceleration resulting from flow around bends is disregarded. This error is small, however, if the tangential velocity is very small.

## The Concept of Tortuosity

In case an irregular tube is not straight but tortuous, equation 7 would not be valid in its present form. Carman (9) proposed a tortuosity factor in order to determine $\overline{u}$ as a function of the potential difference between the ends of a tortuous tube and the straight line distance between the two ends. The tortuosity affects the flow in two ways.

The first effect is with respect to $\overline{u}$. If the direction between the two ends of the tube is defined as the x-direction, then $\overline{u}$ varies from point to point because the direction of the axis of the tube varies, i.e., the component of velocity in the x-direction varies even though the magnitude of the velocity remains constant. If $|\underline{v}|$ is the magnitude of the average tangential velocity, the mean value of the component is given by

$$\overline{u} = |\underline{v}| \frac{L}{L_e} \tag{10}$$

where L is the direct distance between the ends of the tube and $L_e$ is the length of the tortuous tube. The quantity $\underline{v}$ in equation 10 is analogous to $\overline{u}$ in equation 7.

$$|\overline{\nabla P^*}| = \frac{\Delta P^*}{L} \left[ \frac{L}{L_e} \right] \tag{11}$$
