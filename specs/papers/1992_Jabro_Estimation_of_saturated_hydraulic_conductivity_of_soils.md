# ABSTRACT

Since laboratory and field measurement of soil hydraulic properties is time consuming and subject to large error, numerous models have been proposed to predict soil hydraulic properties from easily measurable soil properties such as particle size distribution, bulk density, effective porosity and carbon content. In this study a multiple linear regression model was developed to predict the saturated hydraulic conductivity of soils from their particle size distribution and bulk density data. Published data from 350 soil core samples of varying soils from different sources were used to develop the model. Stepwise regression selected the best model for prediction of soil hydraulic conductivity (R² = 0.68, P < 0.0001) from the independent parameters of silt, clay, and bulk density. Additional field measured data were collected to test and validate the model using several statistical evaluation procedures. Based on the statistical evaluation criteria, the model performed fairly well and gave a satisfactory validation versus the field measured data.

KEYWORDS. Soil hydraulic conductivity, Particle size distribution, Bulk density, Stepwise regression, Model validation.

# INTRODUCTION

Saturated hydraulic conductivity, Ks, is the measure of a soil's ability to transmit water under saturated conditions (Klute and Dirksen, 1986). It is a function of particle size distribution, pore size distribution, pore continuity and configuration, and bulk density. Ks is an important parameter used in determining infiltration, irrigation practice, drainage design, runoff, groundwater recharge, and in simulating leaching and other agricultural and hydrological processes. Measurement of Ks in the field is costly, time consuming, and relatively cumbersome. Additionally, field soils exhibit large spatial variabilities in their hydraulic properties, especially their hydraulic conductivities. This variability implies that a large number of field measurements may be required to characterize a given field or an area.

To date, no single method for measuring Ks is satisfactory for all soils under all conditions, because all methods commonly give variable results. The Guelph permeameter (GP) has been proposed by Reynolds and

Elrick (1985) as a tool to measure Ks. Chief among its limitations is the fact that the GP gives negative and positive values and produces substantial variability within a given soil. The GP can be used on homogeneous horizons, structurally stable soils, and sandy or coarse loamy textured soils, however, it cannot be used on fine, textured, organic, wet, and heterogeneous soils.

Therefore, it is necessary and useful to develop simplified empirical models to estimate the saturated hydraulic conductivity of soils from other easily measurable soil physical properties. Numerous attempts have been made to predict soil hydraulic properties from other soil characteristics, such as particle size distribution, bulk density, effective porosity, and carbon content using empirical equations (Gupta and Larson, 1979; Arya and Paris, 1981; Rawls and Brakensiek, 1982; Rawls, 1983; Puckett et al., 1985; Haverkamp and Parlange, 1986; Wosten and Van Genuchten, 1988; Ahuja et al., 1989; Vereecken et al., 1989; Wu et al., 1990; Franzmeier, 1991). Recently, Wagenet and Hutson (1989) recognized the value of using easily obtained soil parameters in predicting retentivity properties in the LEACHM model as a management tool, but none have reported a model to estimate saturated hydraulic conductivity.

The specific objective of this work was to establish if there is an empirical relationship that relates saturated hydraulic conductivity to soil bulk density and particle size distribution. Once the relationship was established and a predictive model was developed, additional work was performed to validate that model.

# MATERIALS AND METHODS
DATA COLLECTION AND METHODS

Data on particle size distribution, bulk density and saturated hydraulic conductivity of various soil series were collected from Southern Cooperation Series Bulletins (Dan et al., 1983; Nofziger et al., 1983; Quisenberry et al., 1987). The standard methodology used to produce these data were given in the above references and are repeated here for the reader's benefit. The pipette method (Day, 1965) was used to determine clay content. Sand fraction was separated using wet sieving technique and silt was calculated by difference. Undisturbed soil cores (7.6 cm × 7.6 cm) were taken with an Uhland soil sampler at different depths ranging between 15 and 150 cm. Saturated hydraulic conductivity of soil was measured on undisturbed soil cores using a constant head method (Klute, 1965). The soil cores were oven-dried and bulk density was determined using core method (Blake, 1965).

This study used data from 350 samples of varying particle size distribution, bulk density, and saturated hydraulic conductivity (Table 1).

In addition, to validate the model, particle size distribution, bulk density, and saturated hydraulic conductivity (Table 2) were measured on Duffield silt loam (fine-loamy, mixed, mesic Ultic Hapludalf) at Lancaster County in southeastern Pennsylvania (Jabro et al., 1991 a, b). The soil is well drained, moderately well structured and developed from limestone material. Saturated hydraulic conductivity measurements were taken in-situ on a 10 m × 10 m grid sampling system at 20-40 cm and 40-60 cm depths using the Guelph permeameter (Reynolds and Elrick, 1985). A total of 18 measurements were used to test and validate the model. Undisturbed soil cores (7.62 cm × 7.62 cm) were also obtained at 20-40 cm and 40-60 cm depths using an Uhland soil sampler. Soil bulk density was measured on each core (Blake and Hartge, 1986) and particle size distribution was determined using the hydrometer method (Gee and Bauder, 1986).

### STATISTICAL AND MODEL EVALUATION METHODS

The sand, silt, and clay fractions data were transformed to logarithms because these three parameters are highly correlated (r > -0.90, P < 0.0001). Saturated hydraulic conductivity data were also transformed to logarithms because soil hydraulic conductivity data were found to be better described by the log-normal frequency distribution. Stepwise regression procedure (SAS Institute Inc., 1985) was used to determine the best relationship between the independent variables (sand, silt, clay, and bulk density) and dependent variable (saturated hydraulic conductivity).

Several statistical evaluation procedures were used to test the performance of the model (Addiscott and Whitmore, 1987). These methods included: comparison of the statistics for the measured and predicted results (mean and standard deviation); correlation coefficient (r), which measures the degree of association between measured and predicted values; the mean difference between measured and predicted data (M_d), which measure the degree of coincidence; and the relative error (E_r). The mathematical expressions for the mean difference between the measured and predicted data and the relative error are given in equations 1 and 2, respectively:

$$M_d = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (M_i - P_i) \tag{1}$$

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

### MODEL DEVELOPMENT

Correlation coefficients (r) were calculated for the soil parameters incorporated in the model. Soil bulk density and logarithms of sand, silt, and clay were significantly correlated with log(K_s) (r = -0.55, 0.63, -0.67, and -0.72, respectively, at P < 0.0001).

The stepwise regression was then performed and all four independent parameters sand, silt, clay, and bulk density were included. All the model input parameters were significant (P < 0.0001) except the sand variable (Table 3). $$\log(K_s) = 9.56 - 0.81 \log (\% \text{ silt}) - 1.09 \log (\% \text{ clay}) - 4.64(\text{Bd}) \tag{3}$$

The coefficient of multiple determination ($R^2$) of the model was significant and relatively high ($R^2 = 0.68$, P < 0.0001). The coefficient of determination remained invariable after eliminating the sand parameter from the model. This denotes that sand content in these soils did not play a significant role in predicting soil hydraulic conductivity.

Predicted values of $K_s$ were then plotted on a log-log scale against the measured values (fig. 1). A least-square line (not shown) fitted through a plot of predicted and measured $K_s$ values had a correlation coefficient of 0.83 at P < 0.0001.

### MODEL VALIDATION

The model was tested against measured data of field-saturated hydraulic conductivity, particle size distribution, and bulk density (Table 2) obtained from nine locations at two depths on Duffield silt loam soil (Jabro et al., 1991 a, b). Percentage of silt and clay, and soil bulk density variables were inserted into the model (eq. 3) and estimated $K_s$ results were then compared with the measured values of soil hydraulic conductivity (Table 2). The means and standard deviations of both simulated and measured results were compared and were slightly deviated from each other. Furthermore, a high correlation coefficient (r = 0.79, P < 0.0001) was found between the field measured and model simulated hydraulic conductivities.

A student's t-test was also used to compare the field measurements versus model simulations (Table 2). The test (t = 0.60, P > 0.56) showed that there were no significant differences between the predicted and measured values of saturated soil hydraulic conductivity. A comparison was also made between the mean of measurements and the mean of predictions using the student's t-test for the null hypothesis of no difference (Steel and Torrie, 1980). The test indicated that there was no statistically significant difference between the two means at the 0.01 probability level. Graphic depiction (fig. 2) showed that the data points are uniformly scattered around the 1:1 line. This statistically reasonable agreement between the measured and modeled results (fig. 2) suggests that the model (eq. 3) performed well, thus the model was satisfactorily validated. Although this model may be useful only for soils with characteristics similar to the calibration set (Dan et al., 1983; Nofziger et al., 1983; Quisenberry et al., 1987), the importance of $K_s$ data acquisition dictates the need of estimating this property by less expensive methods. Furthermore, the model may not be valid for soils with high contents of exchangeable sodium, soluble salts, clay, and organic matter. Further investigation is required to test the model with other specific soils and site conditions.

### CONCLUSIONS

One can conclude from this study that an empirical relationship exists between saturated hydraulic conductivity and easily measurable soil parameters such as particle size distribution and bulk density. The obvious advantage of using such a predictive model arises from the fact that actual measurement of $K_s$ is time consuming and costly. Additionally, the model should be useful for estimating $K_s$ for modeling, planning, and management purposes where $K_s$ data are not available and direct $K_s$ measurements are not feasible.
