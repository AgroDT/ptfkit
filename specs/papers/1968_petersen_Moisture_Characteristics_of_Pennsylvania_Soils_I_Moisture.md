# Moisture Characteristics of Pennsylvania Soils: I. Moisture Retention as Related to Texture$^{1}$

G. W. PETERSEN, R. L. CUNNINGHAM, AND R. P. MATELSKI$^{2}$

## ABSTRACT

Soil moisture retentions are associated with similar soil properties within each textural class of the USDA Textural Classification and family grouping of the New Classification System. Multiple regression analyses showed bulk density most strongly associated with 1/3 atm moisture (core samples) and clay content with 15 atm moisture (<2 mm sieved samples). Organic carbon was also associated with 15 atm moisture but not with 1/3 atm moisture. Available moisture, as determined by the difference between 1/3 atm and 15 atm moisture, was negatively correlated with sand and clay and positively correlated with silt content. Available moisture was also highly correlated with 1/3 atm moisture whereas 15 atm moisture showed either no correlation or a negative correlation.

Scatter diagrams, with corresponding quadratic equations

for the line of best fit, allow determinations of 1/3-atm and 15-atm moisture contents from known clay percentages. Prediction equations, for estimating available moisture from predetermined soil properties, are given for each textural class, with the exception of sand and sandy clay, and for each textural class of family grouping in the New Classification System.

*Additional Key Words for Indexing:* soil family groupings.

THE INFLUENCE of textural composition on soil properties suggest that soil moisture relationships be studied within each USDA soil textural class (19). Moisture characteristics within the family groupings of the New Classification System (20, 21) also warrant investigation because of the increased use of this system. From a knowledge of relationships between moisture characteristics and soil properties within textural classes, it may be possible to estimate soil moisture characteristics in specific soils where moisture information is unavailable.

Various soil properties have been related to moisture characteristics in an effort to determine the factors controlling soil moisture regimes. Clay contents have been positively correlated with moisture percentages at 1/3 atm and 15 atm soil moisture tensions (1), but have either shown no correlation (11) or a negative correlation (8) with available moisture as determined by the difference between 1/3 and 15 atm moisture percentage. Sand percentages have been correlated with field capacity (25), but have shown negative correlations with available moisture (8, 11). These investigators concluded that silt particles were of primary importance in controlling available moisture in soils.

Positive correlations of organic matter with available moisture have been obtained by Hill (5). This influence of organic matter, however, appears to be related to texture (8, 18) and in some instances organic matter favors soil aggregation and structural development, which may result in a reduction of available moisture retained at lower tensions (7, 17). El Ashkar et al. (4) have reported a positive association between iron oxide content and moisture retention at 1/3 and 15-atm tensions but these results were not supported by Prebble and Stirk (14).

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

Soils were collected from 27 counties in Pennsylvania for laboratory characterization by the Pennsylvania State University Soil Characterization Laboratory in conjunction with the Pennsylvania Soil Survey Program. These soils, representing a broad spectrum of Pennsylvania's soil series, were described, each horizon sampled, and triplicate cores extracted with a modified 2-inch by 1-inch Uhland (23) core sampler.

Organic matter was determined by a modified Walkley-Black wet oxidation procedure (13). Particle size analyses were made by the pipette method of Kilmer and Alexander (9) with the modifications suggested by Kilmer and Mullins (10) using sodium hexametaphosphate (Calgon) as the dispersing agent. The soils were categorized into their respective USDA textural classes, and samples selected from each class, using a random numbers table, to obtain a group of samples with an equal representation of each textural class and covering the range of textural classes found in Pennsylvania.

One-third atm moisture percentages of "undisturbed cores" (16) and 15-atm moisture percentages of < 2 mm sieved samples (15) were obtained as outlined in USDA Handbook no.

60. (24, p. 109-110). Percentage available moisture was obtained by subtracting 15-atm percentages from 1/3-atm percentages. Moisture weight percentages for each sample were converted to a volume basis by multiplication by their respective core bulk densities.

Statistical analyses were carried out on the IBM 7074 System using a multiple regression program with a parsimony option (3).

The following symbols are used for the designated terminology.

$W_{1/3}$ Moisture content of "undisturbed cores" after equilibration at 1/3 atm tension

$W_{15}$ Moisture content of < 2 mm sieved soil material after equilibration at 15 atm tension

$W_A$ Available soil moisture as calculated by $W_{1/3} - W_{15}$

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Average moisture contents at 1/3 and 15 atm levels and available moisture for various textural classes (Table 1) were determined from 1,267 surface and subsoil samples considered representative of Pennsylvania's soil textural classes. These moisture contents are expressed as percent by volume as this expression more meaningfully expresses water available to plant rooting systems. The adequacy of 1/3 atm moisture content as an estimate of field capacity in coarse-textured soils may be debated and 1/10 atm tensions may be more appropriate. This problem was not pursued in this study and 1/3 atm tensions were used on all soils.

The USDA textural classes in Table 1 are arranged from coarse to fine. The values of $W_A$ are lowest in the sand-rich coarse-textured classes, increase in the clay-rich fine-textured classes, and reach a maximum in the silt-rich medium-textured classes. Decreases in available moisture are evident in samples containing >70% sand or more than 40% clay. A regrouping of the USDA textural classes into textural classes for family groups in the New Classification System (20, 21) results in the coarse silty group having the highest average $W_A$ followed by the fine silty group. This indicates that within the silt loam textural class $W_A$ continues to increase with increasing silt. Coarse textures have low Wₐ because they have pores too large to retain much moisture as indicated by their low W₁₅. As textures become finer Wₐ increases because more pores are present with dimensions favorable for holding water available for plant use. However, as textures become increasingly finer Wₐ decreases because more water is retained by the increased surface area and points of contact which harbor water as films held at high tensions. These relationships are evident when W₁₅ and W₁₅ are plotted for each textural class arranged from coarse to increasingly finer texture (Fig. 1). In general, as textures become finer W₁₅ and W₁₅ increase. However, W₁₅ increases at a greater rate for the finer textured classes resulting in the greatest Wₐ in the medium-textured classes. These relationships are similar to the theoretical values reported by Buckman and Brady (2, p. 176).

Each textural class was submitted to multiple regression analysis (22, p. 277–304) to determine soil factors which influence the parameters W₁₅ and W₁₅ (Table 2). In these multiple regression analyses, each variable was considered independent of the others and no attempt was made to derive mechanistic equations for soil moisture retentions. In all textural classes of the USDA Classification, bulk density strongly influences W₁₅, except in loamy sands; clay strongly influences W₁₅, except in clay loams. Within the New Classification System bulk density also showed the strongest association with W₁₅ in all classes, except sandy and coarse silty, and clay the strongest association with W₁₅ in all classes except very fine clayey. Therefore, with the exception of the coarse-textured soils where sand is the most highly associated with W₁₅, as bulk density increases W₁₅ decreases and as clay content increases W₁₅ increases until the extremely high clay levels of the very fine clayey class are reached where silt content is the only factor significant. In this very fine clayey class, silt has a negative influence because the silt particles are surrounded by clay and are not present in great enough proportions to aggregate and form pores. As silt increases within the fine clayey class, more of the soil matrix is composed of these nonpore forming silt particles resulting in lowered moisture retention.

Organic carbon is related to W₁₅, but shows no relationship to W₁₅. Organic matter favors soil aggregation and the formation of large pores which retain little water. Air capacity increases at the expense of moisture storage capacity and reduces the values of W₁₅ and the available moisture storage capacity (6, 8). The ability of organic matter to retain moisture at tensions above the permanent wilting point could account for the relationship of increasing W₁₅ with increasing organic matter.

W₁₅ can be estimated from these quadratic equations and Wₐ obtained by difference. The greatest increase in W₁/₃ and W₁₅ with increasing clay percentages occurs in the sandy soils. These soils are so low in clay, and in their corresponding values of W₁/₃ and W₁₅, that any increase in clay would improve their water-holding capacities. However, at high clay percentages, increases in clay do not result in proportional increases in W₁/₃ and W₁₅. This is especially evident in Fig. 3 where increases in clay above 60% result in little or no change in W₁/₃, and in the very fine clayey class in Table 2 where silt rather than clay shows the greatest association with W₁₅. The apparent leveling of curve W₁/₃ begins at lower clay contents than for curve W₁₅, and results in the decreases in Wₐ with increasing clay content (Fig. 1).

Another method for estimating Wₐ is from prediction equations using various soil properties. From the equations in Table 3, the most important soil properties for determining Wₐ in coarse-textured soils are sand and silt content; but as textures become finer, bulk density becomes the dominant factor. Within all textural classes of the USDA and the New Classification Systems, either increasing bulk density, sand, or clay results in decreasing Wₐ; whereas, increasing silt results in increasing Wₐ.

In the very fine clayey grouping, any change in clay content would result in only small changes in W_1/2 (Fig. 3) and therefore W_1/2 would be relatively constant within this grouping and its relative importance in a prediction equation would be diminished.

Moisture relationships and methods of predicting moisture retentions are only estimates within a textural class and may be in error for a specific soil. Nevertheless, the predictibility of water retentions becomes feasible when laboratory data are available for a sample (i.e., clay content and/or field capacity determination) and it is impossible or impractical to obtain laboratory soil moisture retentions. Soil factors associated with moisture retentions and average values for a given textural class are also a valuable aid in field estimations of available water. Field moisture retentions, however, are not only influenced by the properties of individual horizons, but also by morphological features in the soil profile. Work is in progress to relate soil moisture characteristics to the soil profile and to soil classification units.
