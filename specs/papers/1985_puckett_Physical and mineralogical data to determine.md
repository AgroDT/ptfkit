# Physical and Mineralogical Data to Determine Soil Hydraulic Properties¹

W. E. PUCKETT, J. H. DANE, AND B. F. HAJEK²

A study was conducted to determine relationships among physical, mineralogical, and soil hydraulic properties of Ultisols developed in unconsolidated sediments of the Lower Coastal Plain. Soil water retention curves, saturated hydraulic conductivity, bulk density, quantitative mineralogy, surface area, and particle size distribution were determined by horizon for seven pedons and relationships among them were analyzed by regression analysis procedures. Fine sand, sand and clay percentages were highly correlated with saturated hydraulic conductivity, surface area and volumetric water contents at specific pressure heads. Correlation coefficients > 0.95 were obtained for regression equations estimating soil water retention curves from particle size data. These relationships can be useful for quick characterization of soil hydraulic properties from particle size distribution data. No significant correlations were found between soil hydraulic and mineralogical properties of the clay fraction.

**Additional Index Words:** soil water retention, saturated hydraulic conductivity, bulk density, porosity, surface area, particle size distribution.

**K**NOWLEDGE OF a soil's hydraulic properties is of major importance in modern agriculture since these properties influence plant growth, soil aeration, soil temperature, drainage, irrigation, and trafficability. Measurement of these properties is, however, expensive and time consuming. The need exists, therefore, to predict these properties from easily and routinely obtainable data such as particle size distribution.

The most common parameters to predict soil-water content at high soil-water pressure head ($h$) values have been pore space (Visser, 1966), pore volumes and pore radii corresponding to particle size fractions (Arya and Paris, 1981), and bulk density (Petersen et al., 1968). At low soil-water pressure head values the more common parameters have been clay content (Lund, 1959; Petersen et al., 1968; Banin and Amiel, 1969), and clay plus humus (Visser, 1966) or organic matter content (Gupta and Larson, 1979). Surface area, commonly determined to identify clay minerals in soils (Hajek and Dixon, 1966; Karathanasis and Hajek, 1982a) was found to be very closely related to clay content and water retention values at $h = -33$ kPa (Hillel, 1971; Mortland, 1954). Except as reported by Arya and Paris (1981), relations between physical soil properties and water content values at given $h$-values are based on regression analysis (Brakensiek et al., 1981; Gupta and Larson, 1979; Petersen et al., 1968). Many of the reported relations, however, include the results of disturbed soil samples and may therefore not be applicable to field situations.

The saturated hydraulic conductivity ($K_s$) value of a soil horizon or pedon is required in the analysis of any saturated-soil water-flow system. The $K_s$ relates to surface ponding, runoff, erosion, and the suitability of the soil to serve as septic tank filter fields. Measurement of $K_s$ is both expensive and time consuming, not so much in laboratory time as in field collection of the samples. Estimating $K_s$ from soil morphological or other easily obtainable soil properties may not be very accurate at a given point but still quite satisfactory when applied to large soil areas.

Anderson and Bouma (1973) and Bouma et al. (1979) estimated $K_s$ values from morphometric (sizes and patterns of soil voids) soil data using soil peels, but found that predicting $K_s$ of clay soils from a field description of soil structure was difficult due to the small number of voids which apparently contribute to flow. King and Franzmeier (1981) were able to relate $K_s$ values of Indiana subsoils to texture, origin of parent material, and genetic horizon. McKeague et al. (1982) developed $K_s$ classes for soil horizons by relating structure, porosity, biopores, texture, consistence and density to measured $K_s$ values.

The objectives of this study were (i) to characterize the physical, mineralogical, and hydraulic properties of selected Lower Coastal Plain soils, (ii) to quantify relationships among and between the physical, mineralogical and soil hydraulic properties, and (iii) to select equations that are useful for predicting of soil hydraulic properties.

Six soils (Table 1) were sampled during the summer of 1981 at seven locations in the Lower Coastal Plain of Alabama (approximately 35 000 km²). Sites were selected and described by Soil Conservation Service personnel.

Four undisturbed core samples ($d = 53.5$ mm and $h = 60$ mm) were taken from each horizon at each location with a drop-hammer type sampler. The core samples were treated with propylene oxide and refrigerated to prevent fungal and bacterial growth until needed.

The bulk sample from each horizon was also used to determine the surface area per unit mass by thermogravimetric analysis of adsorbed water (Karathanasis and Hajek, 1982a). Quantitative mineralogy was determined for each sample using the procedure outlined by Karathanasis and Hajek (1982b).

The 60-mm high core samples were used to obtain water retention at soil-water pressure head values of 0, -1, -2, -4, -8, -15, -40, -80, -200, and -1000 kPa. Tempe pressure cells (Reginato and van Bavel, 1962), with modifications according to Tanner and Elrick (1958), were used over the air pressure range from 1 to 80 kPa. A 300-kPa pressure plate extractor was used for the 200 kPa pressure,

and a 1500-kPa ceramic plate extractor was used for the 1000-kPa applied pressure.

Curves were fitted through the pressure head-water content values using a nonlinear least-squares method (van Genuchten, 1978). The equation used was

$$\theta = \theta_r + (\theta_s - \theta_r)/[1 + (\alpha|h|)^n]^m, \quad [1]$$

where the subscripts s and r refer to saturated and residual values of volumetric water content (θ), h is the soil water pressure head, and α, n and m = 1 - 1/n are parameters that depend on the shape of the θ(h) curve. Replicate core values were averaged to obtain the θ(h) relationship for each horizon.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Results of analyses on the disturbed soil samples, viz., surface area, particle size distribution and mineralogy, are presented in Table 2. The surface area increases with increasing depth, which corresponds

with increasing clay content, but begins to decrease when the clay content decreases in the lower part of the profile.

Despite the large differences in particle size distributions among the six soil series (seven locations), e.g., the clay content varied from a low of 1.4% in the E4 horizon of the Troup to a high of 42.1% in the Bt4 of the Dothan soil, the mineralogical composition of the clay fraction did not differ substantially from soil to soil. In all cases, hydroxy interlayered vermiculite (HIV), kaolinite and gibbsite were the minerals present in the greatest abundance.

The $\theta_s$ values refer to those water content values obtained at $h=0$ and are usually not equal to the total porosity due to the entrapment of air. The parameters $\alpha$, $n$, and $\theta_r$ in Table 3 specify the water retention curves (Eq [1]; van Genuchten, 1978) as fitted through the experimentally determined data points. The Ap horizon of the Lucedale soil (Fig. 1a) illustrates a good fit based on the residuals between the measured and fitted water contents while the E3 horizon of the Troup soil (Fig. 1b) resulted in the worst fit, with deviations as high as 0.04 between measured and fitted water content values. It should be emphasized that the van Genuchten (1978) procedure only fits a curve through the experimental data points and is not a predictive method of some sort.

The values of $\alpha$ were independent of particle size and followed no observable pattern as was also pointed out by van Genuchten (1978). The values of $n$ correlated with sand content ($n = -0.057 + 0.028$ x% sand, $r = .80$), and assumed their highest values for the Troup soil. Knowledge of approximate values of $n$ is useful in parameter estimation problems as discussed by Dane and Hruska (1983).

The $K_s$ values varied considerably, and ranged from $8.33 \times 10^{-10}$ (Bt2, Orangeburg) to $3.94 \times 10^{-5}$ (E1, Troup) ms$^{-1}$. Saturated conductivity was highest in the coarse textured soils and lowest in dense argillic horizons.

The bulk density values ranged from 1.47 (Ap, Malbis 2) to 1.86 (Ap, Dothan) Mg m$^{-3}$. The average bulk density for all soils was $> 1.63$ Mg m$^{-3}$, which could be a detrimental factor affecting root growth in Lower Coastal Plain soils (Tackett and Pearson, 1964; Eavis and Payne, 1969; Camp and Lund, 1968).

### Water Retention Curves

Volumetric water content values used in the regression analysis were taken from the fitted data curves (Eq. [1]). Regression models for estimating soil-water-retention curves from particle-size distribution, bulk density and porosity are presented in Table 4. The models are given by

$$\theta_{p_1} = a \times \text{bulk density} + b \times \text{porosity} + c$$

$$\theta_{p_2} = d \times \% \text{fine sand} + e \times \% \text{sand} + f$$

$$\times \% \text{clay} + g,$$

where $\theta_{p_1}$ is the predicted water content (m$^3$ m$^{-3}$) at $h = 0$ or $h = -1$ kPa, $\theta_{p_2}$ is the predicted water content (m$^3$ m$^{-3}$) at all $h$-values $< -1$ kPa, the coefficients $a$, $b$, $d$, $e$, and $f$ are regression coefficients, and $c$ and $g$ are constants. Bulk density is in Mg m$^{-3}$, porosity is a dimensionless quantity (m$^3$ m$^{-3}$) and fine sand, sand and clay are expressed in percentages on a total mass basis. The data used in developing these regression equations included soils with a range of sand (35-89%), silt (7-36%), clay (1-42%), bulk density (1.47-1.86 Mg m$^{-3}$) and porosity (0.30-0.48) values. Although the latter two properties were highly correlated, the use of both improved the results. This was attributed to the use of the measured particle density ($\rho_p$) values to calculate the porosity ($\epsilon = 1 - \rho_b/\rho_p$) rather than one value for all soils. Because of its low content, organic matter was not used in developing these equations, which is in contrast with Gupta and Larson (1979) and Rawls et al. (1982).

Because of the limited effect of particle size near saturation, the volumetric water contents at $h$-values $\ge -1$ kPa were calculated from bulk density ($\rho_b$) and porosity ($\epsilon$) values.

### Saturated Conductivity

The K_s exhibited extreme variability and was difficult to predict. Although linear and multiple regression procedures produced equations that had high r²-values, in some cases they predicted negative values for K_s. The nonlinear regression procedure gave the best estimates for K_s as shown in Fig. 3. Three points deviated considerably from the bulk of the data points, viz. the Malbis 2 Ap, and the Bt1 and Bt2 of the Cahaba soil. These three horizons had porosity values > 0.42. The curve in Fig. 3 can be divided into three parts with breaks at 10 and 20% clay. The first area reflects the sand and loamy sand textures, the second area the sandy loam textures, and the third area the sandy clay loam and heavier soils.

### Surface Area

The hypothesis that surface area per unit volume, which takes into account the density of the soil, should relate better to other soil properties than the surface area on a mass basis was not supported by statistical evidence. Both types of surface areas related equally well to K_s, water retention, and percentage of fine sand, sand, and clay.

### CONCLUSION

In conclusion, useful regression equations were developed for predicting the soil water pressure headwater content relationships of soils in the Lower Coastal Plain, using particle size data, bulk density and porosity values. The saturated hydraulic conductivity was best estimated from the percentage of clay.

It should be emphasized that the mineralogical composition of the clay fraction of these soils was very similar, consisting mainly of hydroxy interlayered vermiculite, kaolinite, and gibbsite. Quantities did not differ enough to cause significantly different correlations with measured soil hydraulic properties. The excellent fit between the measured values and those predicted from regression equations, using particle size, bulk density, and porosity, should be viewed in this light together with the similar genesis of the soils in the Lower Coastal Plain. Since the relationships presented in this study differ from those previously reported, it seems reasonable that useful relationships between soil hydraulic properties and easily obtainable or existing information, such as soil texture, should be developed for areas in which the soils have similar mineralogy and genesis.
